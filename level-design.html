I Start here

Gameplay first, and know why

A map is a set of choices for the players in it: where to go, what to take, where to stand, when to run. This guide is about making those choices interesting. It's written for duels first, because a layout that holds up between two good players holds up nearly everywhere, with notes for free for all and team play where they differ.

None of it is law. Every rule here has a famous map that breaks it. DM4 is lopsided, keeps its Mega in a dead end and drops players in front of their killer, and it was the duel map for years. Learn why a rule exists, break it on purpose, and let good players tell you whether it worked.

Looks matter too. A handsome map gets downloaded; only a map that plays well stays installed. You don't have to choose between them, only make the decisions in the right order: the layout, then the items, then the paint.

Oz's own game plays the original 1.32 rules, with movement in fixed 8 ms steps and the auto hop, so a jump is the same at every frame rate. Every number in this guide is Oz's, measured in the game or read from its code. A map travels, though: the same .map plays on CPMA and other community servers under their own movement, so if that's where it will live, test it there too.

II The process

Paper, blockout, players, then paint

Decide first what the map is for. A serious duel map is built differently from a fun one: every decision is weighed by how it changes the fight, and it goes through round after round of testing with players who know what they're doing. A fun map can spend more of its effort on the look.

Start on paper

Don't open the editor and improvise outward from one cool room. Layouts made that way rarely have the depth a duel needs, and you only find out after the work is done. Draw a flow map first: big rooms as boxes, balconies as smaller boxes stuck to them, halls as lines, stairs as railroad tracks, lifts and pads as dots. It needn't hold every detail, only the rooms, how they connect and roughly how high each part is. Sketch a few maps you like the same way while you run them: you'll see how differently they're built, and that the best ones are surprisingly hard to draw.

Block it out

Turn the drawing into plain brushes: floors, walls, the main heights, the items and the spawns. No texturing, no detail, no careful light. This is where the scale gets settled, which is the hardest thing to get right. When in doubt, measure a map you like: walk it, count grid squares, note its hall widths and floor heights.

In the editor, Brush (B) draws boxes on the grid (1 to 9 set its size) and Hollow makes a room of one. The Jump pad (J) and Teleport (K) tools place working ones in two clicks. Play (F5) compiles quickly and drops you in, Play from here (Shift+F5) starts you where the camera is, and the pause menu's Back to the map editor returns you to the map as you left it. The compile walls the map in sky by itself, so an unfinished blockout still runs, but anything that falls off an open edge dies.

Test the skeleton

Give the blockout to players who play duels seriously, and watch them play it. Ask them about the layout, not the looks: they're the ones who know how a map plays. Other mappers are better at the technical side. If the verdict is that the layout doesn't work, reshape it or scrap it. That's what a blockout is for, and it's cheap now. Big changes to a weak first layout are often exactly what turns it into a good one.

Tune, then dress

Items, spawns and jumps take several rounds of testing to settle. Only then: textures, lighting, detail and clipping. Then test again, because detail changes play. A dark corner becomes a camping spot, a light fixture snags players mid-fight, a pillar blocks a sightline that used to matter.

The beat of a fight

A fight is a run of shots, and between two shots of the same gun a player moves a fixed distance. Call it the beat. Within one beat the ground should offer several different moves: a step up, a pillar, a doorway, a drop. A long tight corridor offers only forward and back, so whoever arrives with more health and the better gun wins, whatever their skill. A huge empty room offers so many equal moves that none of them matter. Combat space lives between the two.

IV Height, flow and links

Height, flow and connections

Use the height

Higher ground is the oldest advantage there is. From above, more of the map is in view. The floor under an enemy catches your rockets' splash. You can drop to any lower level when you like, while the player below has only the ways up. And one step back from the edge puts the floor between you. A good map hands out that advantage, then makes it cost something.

Spread tiers sideways instead of stacking them. Floors piled straight on top of each other only meet through holes. Offset like terraces, every level looks into the others, and fights cross between them.

Then give the low player answers: ceilings low enough over an upper walkway that rockets fired from below splash into it, a grate that lets splash through, more than one way up. High ground should be an advantage that changes hands, never a seat for the rest of the match.

Keep it flowing

On the large scale a map should loop. A player can keep running, never turning fully around, and come back to where they started having passed most of the interesting areas. A bare ring isn't enough, though: with no crosscut two players just chase each other around a track. Add at least one way across the middle.

Avoid whatever stops a player cold: a lift that takes forever, a long empty hall, a staircase of more than three dozen steps with no landing, a floor cluttered with props that snag. Every area will be fought in at some point, so make each one a little interesting: a low step, a platform, a pillar to break line of sight. Items keep players moving too. Put ammo a short run from its gun rather than on top of it: a player standing on a pile of goodies has no reason to go anywhere.

Connect it well

Connectivity is how easily a player gets from one place to another: how direct the routes are, how many ways lead in and out of a room, how quickly a newcomer learns the shape of it.

  • No room with only one door. If a room must have one entrance, give it an exit that's only an exit: a drop, a window, a one-way teleporter.
  • One connection of each kind. A second door from the same spot to the same spot adds nothing. A connection from a different part to a different part (a balcony to a floor, a ledge to a hall) adds a real choice.
  • Direct routes. From anywhere there should be a fairly direct way to anywhere else, usually through the middle. Nobody should need a grand tour to cross the map.
  • Rooms that flow into rooms. A map of rooms joined by single halls (room, hall, room) turns every hall into a choke point and every fight into a doorway standoff.
  • But not Swiss cheese. With a hole in every wall nothing is worth holding, and the layout stops meaning anything.

And keep it learnable. A player without a map should work out where things are within a run or two. Areas that feel far apart but turn out to sit side by side, unconnected, are the opposite of good connectivity: they confuse instead of offering choices.

V Balance and pull

Balance, imbalance and pull

A perfectly balanced map, where every spot is worth the same, is dull: nothing to fight over and no reason to be anywhere. A wildly unbalanced one is dull too, because the first player to take control keeps it. Aim between the two: enough pull toward some places to start fights, and always a way back for the player who's behind.

The layout pulls first

Before a single item goes in, the shape of the map already moves players around:

  • The center. The more routes run through a room, the more players pass through it. In a three-by-three grid of rooms the middle one has four neighbors, the sides three and the corners two, so the middle sees the most action. Take the middle out and the eight rooms of the ring are all equal again.
  • Gravity. Dropping down is free and climbing costs time, so action drifts downward wherever players can fall from one area to another. Stairs are neutral, though a single stairwell is a bottleneck however neutral it is. Lifts push action back up: easy to ride up, awkward to come down.
  • Slow routes. A crawlway, a long climb or a slow lift starves whatever lies behind it, and the rooms beside the fast routes get busier.

Items pull hardest

Picture the floor plan as a rubber sheet, with the players as balls rolling across it. Every item makes a sag, and the stronger the item the deeper the sag: a Mega pulls far harder than a box of shells. An item that's been taken still pulls, because nobody can see that it's gone. So placing items is placing balance: you can deepen one room's pull, or spread the sags so that no single spot holds everything worth having.

  • Don't bunch strong things. A launcher with three boxes of rockets beside it hands the map to whoever gets there first after it comes back.
  • Don't paper over the layout with items. A room nobody visits because it's hard to reach isn't fixed by a gun in it; fix the routes.
  • Make power cost something. A jump, a risky drop, an exposed spot. But never an ordeal nobody will attempt.

Not symmetrical

A map that's the same on both halves has only half a map of ideas in it, and both halves are worth the same, so there's no middle and nothing in particular to fight over. The Proving Grounds (q3tourney2) gets away with its symmetry only because its items aren't symmetrical: ten extra shards in one of its two main rooms make that room the one to hold. Symmetry within one room, or in a single feature, is fine.

A center worth holding

Imagine six identical rooms, each with a rocket launcher and a health, each with a teleporter to every other. Finding your opponent would be pure luck, and there'd be no strategy at all. Real duel maps have strong areas and weak ones. Holding the strong areas is aggressive play, lurking in the weak ones defensive, and guessing which one your opponent is doing, and when they'll switch, is the deepest skill a duel has. Good maps make that guess matter.

VI Layout families

Layout families that work

Most successful duel maps fall into a few families. They're starting points, not molds: plenty of good maps mix them, and some fit none.

One atrium

One big room on many levels, often four or more, with smaller areas around it. The outer loop sees quick close fights; the atrium sees mid-range hide and seek across its ledges, and players pour into it for the big fights. The armors usually sit on opposite sides of the loop, with a big item, often the Mega, in the atrium's depths.

Two atriums

The champion of the competitive era: two big rooms joined at a corner, three or so tiers each, with halls, stairs and teleporters winding between them. A launcher and an armor in each atrium suits a game of two players, and the flow runs in a figure eight that keeps both moving. The danger is armor running: one player crossing back and forth to take both armors. Put the armors in different atriums and on different floors so a run costs something.

Three atriums

More strategic and slower: a third room, often the largest, in the middle. The footprint is bigger, so there are fewer floors, longer sightlines and more time between sightings, and the railgun matters more. The armors go in the two side rooms, spread as far apart as the map allows; the middle becomes the main battleground, or a quiet place to regroup.

The others

Older catalogs add more: rooms of every size jumbled together and fused where they overlap, with floors staggered half a level apart; a tangle of halls that rise, dip and cross over each other, widening into small rooms where they meet; one big arena room with walkways round it. And one that rarely works: room, corridor, room. If your plan looks like that, turn the rooms into atriums, widen the halls into rooms of their own, and use the height.

Dead ends

Usually bad: they stop the flow and invite camping. A short one with something worth the risk is another matter. DM4's Mega room was a dead end, and going in for the Mega meant being trapped below an opponent with rockets. That trade, a strong item for being cornered, stages some of the best fights a map can have. If a dead end shouldn't be a trap, give it a quick way out that isn't the way in: a hole to drop in by, a teleporter or a jump out.

VII Control points

Control points

A control point is a spot from which one player can reach most of the map, watch most of its approaches and guard its key items. The player in control holds one; the player behind tries to take it from them, or slip around it. Planning these spots is planning the duel.

A good control point gives its holder several escape routes on their own level, a few drops, and lines of sight to the items and entrances that matter. Then it gives them a weakness: an item it can't watch, so going for it means leaving; a jump pad or teleporter that brings the opponent into their face; an exposed approach; a hard way out. A control point with no weakness is a map with one winner.

  • Make the best armor cost the best spot. Taking it should mean leaving the control point for a moment. That moment is the down player's chance.
  • Look at it from below. What can the player who's behind do about the holder? Splash them from under a low ceiling, come out of a teleporter behind them, fight for a different item.
  • Have more than one. Two or three good control points, each covering different items, make control something that moves around the map.

VIII Combat terrain

Ground to fight on

The space where players actually shoot at each other needs a few things: choices of where to move, things to hide behind, surfaces that make the weapons interesting, and nothing that turns a fight into luck.

Cover

Picture The Proving Grounds without the big pillar in its rocket launcher room. The best fights on that map happen around that pillar. Every good duel map has pieces of cover to fight around: pillars, corners, narrow doorways, crenellated walls that hide a player for a stride and then show them, short walls that screen part of a hall so a player can regroup or double back. Cover shouldn't block the route itself: pile crates against the walls, not across the path.

No needless precision

Aiming has two parts: knowing where to aim, which takes real skill, and hitting the exact spot, which past a point is luck. Moving is the same. Ground that demands needlessly fine aim or footwork rewards luck instead of judgment.

  • No grates or railings with gaps just wide enough for a rocket: whether it gets through becomes chance.
  • Simple shapes where people fight: columns used as cover without bases, straight wall edges, light fixtures flush with the wall.
  • Clip whatever snags a running player: door posts, beams, sticking-out trim. And make the clipping match what players see: if a ledge looks climbable let them climb it, and don't block off a roof that looks reachable.
  • Smooth bumpy floors with clip, so a player's aim doesn't jolt as they run.

Ambushes

An ambush is a shot from an angle the victim couldn't have known to watch. A doorway in the middle of a wall, with ledges or alcoves on both sides of it inside the room, means anyone coming through can only watch half of what threatens them. The fix is simple to say and hard to build: wherever a player first sees into a new area, they should be able to see all of it at once by looking the right way. T-junctions and dark corners beside entrances have the same problem.

An ambush spot can be fine, even good, when it costs something. A teleporter exit that an opponent can camp is a fair fight if the arriving player has another way in, or a jump that brings them up behind the camper. What's bad is the spot nobody can do anything about.

Corners that fight

A square, flat-walled corner offers one move: step out and be seen. Cut the corner at an angle, break the wall into ribs and gaps, and players can trade shots around it, bank grenades off it and splash rockets into the far leg without giving up their cover.

Long sightlines

The railgun hits instantly at any range, so long open sightlines are where it rules, and a map whose main areas are all long open views becomes a contest of rail aim and nothing else. Break long sightlines with a bend, a pillar, a change of floor; that also helps the frame rate. Put in enough cover and enough tight space that the rocket launcher, the lightning gun and positioning matter as much as railing.

Oz's own game has lag compensation: an instant-hit shot lands where the shooter saw the target, whatever their ping. That makes the railgun, machine gun and lightning gun as dependable online as on a LAN, so long sightlines weigh even more on Oz's servers than on many others. Test your rail lines online, not only alone.

Light the floor

Mood is fine; not being able to see your opponent isn't. Keep the floor lit everywhere, and never leave a dark corner beside an item or a doorway. Many competitive players run settings that flatten shadows anyway, so a dark area mostly punishes the players who see the map the way you lit it.

Hazards

Lava and slime can raise the stakes in an area. Use them sparingly, keep them shallow, and put them where they make a strong item riskier rather than everywhere. Decide how much they hurt and how hard they are to climb out of. A pit with no way out but death, or a void that swallows a player stacked with armor, frustrates far more than it adds. Traps rarely help a duel: they make for slow, gimmicky play.

The editor's compile puts a deadly drop under the whole map, so any open edge you leave is a void. If an edge shouldn't kill, wall it or clip it.

IX Between floors

Getting between floors

Each way of changing height plays differently: it makes a noise or it doesn't, it can be chased or blocked or it can't, and it leaves the player exposed for a different stretch of time. Use a mix.

Stairs

The default for small changes of height: quiet, smooth, easy to dodge on, and they never stop a fight. Keep each flight short; past half a floor or so, think about a lift, a teleporter or a pad. Pick a step height and keep it everywhere: 8 units (id's choice), 12 or 16, always under the 18-unit step. Under CPMA, stairs and the ramps beside them also launch double jumps, so trim along a staircase creates trick routes.

Teleporters

The best way to join two areas that can't see or hear each other, or to get from the bottom of a map to the top fast. They keep play moving better than jump pads, and the classic duel maps used plenty: DM4 had five, Aerowalk four. Their costs: players have to learn where each one goes, and two-way pairs breed telefrags.

  • One-way teleporters make attacks all or nothing. Through a two-way pair a player can pop through, fire and pop back before anyone reacts: slower, sneakier play, and more telefrags.
  • Space a pair's exits. Put each exit a jump away from the other teleporter, so there's room to fight between arriving and leaving.
  • Keep exits off the wall and on the floor. Off the wall, so a waiting player can stand behind it without being telefragged; on the floor, not in mid-air, so players can jump straight out.
  • Make arriving a gamble. Coming out on high ground is a strength, unless the other player already holds higher ground and knows exactly where you'll appear.

Jump pads

Fast, and fun to misuse, but they make stop-and-go flow, and a player flying along a predictable arc is a free target for a railgun.

  • Cover part of the flight. A pad whose arc runs behind walls or up a shaft is exposed only at its ends.
  • Use the right tool for the height. Half a floor wants stairs; the full height of the map wants a teleporter.
  • Land somewhere worth it with no steering at all, from whichever side the player stepped on.
  • Keep the trigger small. An oversized trigger under a ledge bounces players off the ceiling and back onto the pad, and makes it hard to walk down past a pad without being thrown again.
  • In a railgun map, keep arcs low and quick. High, floating arcs are better saved for maps without one.

Select a jump pad in the editor and the inspector shows its throw: launch speed, how high it rises over the pad, how far away it lands and how long it's in the air. Higher and Lower move its target 32 units at a time, and the arc is drawn in the view as you place it.

Lifts

id left lifts out of its own arenas, but they play well. A lift is a great escape, since once it's moving the chaser can't get on; it's a sound cue; it can be held or denied; and a lift that blocks a passage when it's up makes an interesting gate. Make it fast: nobody enjoys a long ride while their opponent lines up a shot. Because a lift is easy to ride up and awkward to come down, it shifts action upward, which is handy where a drop pulls it down.

Doors

A door is a sound cue. Arrive in a room just as a door swings shut and you know which way your opponent went. Doors that open when shot let players fake a route.

X Items

Placing the items

The layout makes the space; the items make the game. Where they go decides which areas matter, which routes players take and how easily one player can take control.

Danger should match value

The better an item, the more it should cost to take: the red armor more dangerous than the Mega, the Mega than the yellow, the yellow than the green. Danger isn't only lava and drops. An item out in the open on the lowest floor, watched from three ledges, is dangerous because of the other player.

  • Spread the armors and the Mega as far apart as the map allows, on different floors, so collecting all of them is hard (but not impossible).
  • Two or three of them is about right for a duel. One makes the map trivial to control and puts every fight in one room; with many more, nobody needs to fight for any.
  • Give the armors the best fighting spaces in the map: height around them, angles to attack from, room for a few shots before anyone reaches the item. Those fights decide matches.
  • The green armor is the down player's item: tuck it in a quiet place to regroup.
  • An armor that's easy to defend (a pit, a room with narrow ways in) lets the player who's behind fight for it even with weak weapons. Such a spot needs especially interesting space around it.

Armor sets

Count the Mega as an armor, worth a little more than a yellow and less than a red. Under CPMA's armor rules, mixed armors are also harder to deny: a player carrying full red armor can't take a yellow without first losing armor.

Time the run

Armor comes back 25 seconds after it's taken. If a player can go from armor to armor to armor and be back at the first in less time than that, they can hold all of them while their opponent gets nothing. Time it in the blockout, running the circuit at a good player's pace. One classic map's two armors were ten seconds apart, so the player in control spent most of the wait camping one of them.

Weapons

The duel set is nearly fixed: shotgun, grenade launcher, rocket launcher and lightning gun almost always, the railgun in most maps, the plasma gun sometimes, the BFG hardly ever. So the question is less which weapons than where.

Weak weapons in out-of-the-way places give a player who just respawned something to fight with on the way to a better one, and make for varied fights: shotgun against rockets, grenades against the lightning gun. Leaving a weapon out on purpose can give a map its flavor; The Proving Grounds has no grenade launcher or plasma gun and plays as a pure offensive scrap. In the original rules the instant-hit weapons, the railgun and machine gun above all, dominate more than they do in CPMA, so be stingy with the railgun in open maps.

Ammo

  • Not beside its weapon. A short run away, so players keep moving.
  • Three boxes for one gun at most. One or two is usually plenty.
  • Group different ammo together. One spot worth visiting beats two to remember.

Health

Most duel maps carry between 100 and 250 health in all, not counting the 5s: a little more in a bigger map, less with a Mega. One classic map with almost 400 made health denial impossible, so every fight was about weapons and armor; another with 125 made retreating to heal pointless.

  • Group it in a few places rather than spreading it evenly, or players pick up health wherever they go and nothing changes hands.
  • Big groups in contested areas, small ones in quiet areas, but never make healing a suicide run for the player who's behind.
  • Two 25s or one 50? A hurt leader takes the 50 and leaves nothing. With two 25s, once they're full the second one stays for their opponent. If testing shows the leader denying health too often, swap 50s for pairs of 25s.
  • After a close fight the winner should be hurting; after a one-sided one, they should be able to top up.

Shards and fives

Small items with big jobs:

  • Sound cues. Put them in groups of different sizes, from two to five, so a player who hears four pickups knows exactly where their opponent is.
  • Weight. Extra shards make a room worth more, as The Proving Grounds' ten shards make one of its two matching rooms the one to hold.
  • The down player's lifeline. A single shard after a respawn can be the difference between surviving a rail or not, and a cluster away from the main routes lets a player build armor while the leader is busy, which breaks up endless armor runs.

Powerups, holdables and secrets

Keep powerups and holdables out of duels. When one player has the quad, the other hides until it runs out, and the match stalls. A medkit saves a player at the moment they should have died; a personal teleporter makes every chase a guess. A map that's also built for free for all can still have them: give them gametype ffa team and Oz's game leaves them out of duels. Secrets don't belong in competitive maps either: knowing where a hidden item is is map trivia, not skill.

XI Spawns

Where players come back

A respawn is a penalty: the player who died comes back with 125 health, a machine gun and nothing else, while the player who killed them keeps everything. Fighting back from there is one of the skills that makes duels interesting. Spawns that drop players in front of their killer take that skill away; spawns that can never be reached make control meaningless.

How Oz's game picks one

The original rules don't choose from all the spawns at random. They skip any spawn a player is standing on, sort the rest by distance from where you died, and pick at random from the farther half. Since your killer is usually near where you died, that tends to send you away from them, but only if the spawns are spread around the map. If most of them cluster on one side, the farther half of the list still includes spawns right beside the fight.

How many, and where

  • The count shapes the match. Few spawns (under about eight) make respawns predictable and spawn kills common, which some maps want: DM4's long frag runs were part of its legend. More spawns give more variety, and more chances of landing in view.
  • Check the farther halves. For each place players die most (around the strongest armor, the Mega, the launchers), list which spawns fall in the farther half from it, and make sure none of them sits in plain view of that area's control points.
  • Out of sight, back to a wall. Put spawns where the main control areas can't see them, off the busiest paths, facing out into a room with a wall behind, near an escape route and a weak weapon.
  • A step from an item, not on it. Grabbing something a few steps away is part of the fun, and a player who spawns on an item may not even notice they took it. Spawns near armors (as in CPM1A and CPM3) give a player who lands there a fighting chance.
  • Check the starting pair. Both players spawn at once when a match begins. If one spot always beats another to the red armor, the first minute decides too much.

Spawn points stand in the editor as fighters, facing the way the spawn faces: turn them out into the room, not at a wall. A spawn's nobots and nohumans keys keep bots or people off it. The initial flag only matters for a listen server's host on their very first spawn, so it's no tool for a fair start.

One answer to unlucky spawns came out of this same community: Hoony proposed starting every point from two fixed spawns, alternating which player gets the stronger one, like serving in tennis. CPMA built a mode around it. A map made for that mode needs two spawns on equal footing, with weapons near each but spread out, so every point starts with a choice.

XII Sound

Sound is half the map

Good players track each other by ear: the thump of a jump pad, a pickup, a door, a teleport, footsteps on metal. A map can make that rich or useless.

  • What carries. In this engine a player hears the game's sounds from the places it counts as possibly visible from where they stand. Rooms that block each other's view let players move quietly; a wide-open map hears everything. So the way you block sightlines is also how you shape hearing.
  • Make areas sound different. Give each room its own mix of items, so a run of sounds (a heavy landing, a weapon, then armor) tells a listener exactly where you are and where you're headed. DM4's rooms each had their own combination.
  • Use doors and lifts as cues. A target_speaker with its global flag plays to the whole map, such as a door that everyone hears open.
  • Leave out ambient loops around the fighting. Players need to hear each other, not the machinery.
  • Surfaces talk. surfaceparm metalsteps gives a floor metal footsteps, a zone players learn to recognize; surfaceparm nosteps silences them, on a clip brush over stairs say, for a map built for stealth.
  • Count the shards. Groups of different sizes turn pickups into positions (see Items).

Oz's 3D sound tells players which direction a sound came from, so distinct sounds on distinct floors and sides of the map are worth even more than they used to be.

XIII Looks, light, speed

Looks that serve the play

Gameplay comes first, but a map that looks unfinished never gets played long enough for anyone to find out it plays well. Make it look good and play well; just never let the look cost the play.

  • Mark the areas. Give each room, floor and route its own textures or colors, so a player knows where they are at a glance from any angle, without relying on items that may already be gone.
  • Build chunky, on a human scale. Features big enough to matter in a fight. A field of player-high blocks makes good fights; blocks a quarter that size or four times it do far less.
  • Lift the ceilings. A high ceiling makes a space feel big without cluttering the floor.
  • Keep lintels high and floors smooth. Players jump everywhere; detail that catches heads and feet is detail in the wrong place.
  • Check it under other settings. Low texture detail and vertex lighting can wash out the features that tell areas apart.
  • Hold the frame rate. Watch the busiest areas with both players in them firing (the plasma gun especially). Keep triangle counts and overdraw down, cut long sightlines between detailed areas, use hint brushes, go easy on many-layered shaders, and try it on more than one PC.
  • Help the bots, if the map should play well against them: simplify the awkward spots with bot clip.

Oz has three renderers: its own new look, and id's original look on OpenGL or Vulkan. Check your map in more than one. In the editor, the Lit preview (F6) shows the compiled light in the view, and after a compile the warnings list points out leaks and missing materials, framing each one when you pick it.

XIV Trick jumps

Trick jumps

A map doesn't need trick jumps, but good ones reward skill with a shortcut, a surprise or a faster item, and make a map more fun to play and to watch.

  • Shortcuts, never the only way. A trick jump to an item is great as one route of several. As the only route, it locks most players out.
  • Let the architecture make them. Stairs, trims, ledges and ramps create jumps players find for themselves, and the ones players discover are better loved than the ones that look placed. A map built with real architecture, not padded walls, grows them by itself.
  • Risk for reward. A jump hard enough to miss now and then, which leaves you exposed when you do, is worth more than a free one.
  • Deny with authority. If a jump must be impossible, make it miss by 16 units or more. Near misses become tricks someone finds after release, and some of them break the map.
  • Teleporter exits on the floor, with nothing overhead, so players can jump straight out with their speed.

Oz's own game moves in fixed 8 ms steps, so a jump works the same for everyone at any frame rate. On servers that move players frame by frame, and under CPMA's movement (double jumps off steps, ramp jumps, air control), the same map allows more: a staircase or a sloped trim can launch a player somewhere you never planned. Test there if your map will be played there.

XV Testing

Testing

Every map needs more testing than its author expects, by better players than its author. Watch several matches between evenly matched players, and at least one between a strong player and a weak one. Then ask:

  • Who controls the map, and from where? Can the player who's behind get back into it?
  • Where do the fights happen? Is there an area nobody visits?
  • Can one player run every armor? Time the circuit against the 25 seconds.
  • Do spawns hand out free kills? Note every spawn kill and where it happened.
  • Does anything snag, stall or confuse? A slow lift, an oversized trigger, a doorway hit mid-jump, a teleporter that leaves players lost.
  • Do the jumps work as meant? The ones you want, for everyone; the ones you don't, by a clear margin.
  • What would the testers change? Ignore the paint at this stage, and listen hardest to the people who play the most duels.

Play (F5) drops you into the map fast enough to test after every change, and Play from here (Shift+F5) starts you at the camera. Each compile also builds the bots' file, so bots can walk the item routes, though bots tell you whether something can be reached, not whether it plays well. And Oz records every match: watch the replay from either player's view to see the fights you weren't in.

Then release a beta to a wider circle, and keep listening after the release. The community will find jumps and strategies no tester did, and the best maps changed because of them.

XVII Sources

Where these ideas come from

This guide is our own writing, but its ideas come from the competitive mapping community of 1997 to 2002, mostly from the reading list of wviperw's design guide for CPMA. Most of those pages are long gone from the web; the links below go to the Internet Archive's copies. Oz's numbers come from Oz's game code (id's GPL release of the 1.32 code, with Oz's changes) and from measurements in the game.

  • Competitive Design Guide · part two wviperw · promode.org · October 2002

    The backbone: planning and testing, layout theory, the three atrium families with worked examples, and item placement weapon by weapon, then architecture, clipping, lighting, spawns and vertical transport. Its first part lives on at cpma-news.org, without its links.

  • How to make Levels for Q3A Tournament Play twoAM · Challenge World · August 2000

    Power points, sound and visibility, jump pads, lifts, teleporters, cover, armor counts and health totals compared across the classic duel maps. Its comments hold Johnny Law's long reply: circular flow with a crosscut, chunky proportions, grates that reverse high ground, and generous jump margins.

  • What makes DM4 a classic map? Barratus and ztn · Challenge World Maps · April 2000

    The DM4 discussion: why a lopsided map with a dead-end Mega became the duel map, ztn's list of ingredients for a duel map, and players arguing over imbalance, risk and the way back.

  • Lunaran's Deathmatch Encyclopedia Matthew "Lunaran" Breit · 1999

    Five essays: Map Balance, Connectivity, Flow, Architecture and Item Placement. Where the grid of rooms, gravity, the rubber sheet and the flow map come from, with his own later notes on what he'd change.

  • Gameplay is King Geit · January 2002

    A rant about priorities: test gameplay with competitive players before the paint, and remember that looks get a map downloaded while gameplay keeps it.

  • Multiplayer Map Design Myrkul (Charles Kendrick) · 1997

    The "HowTo: Level Design" of the reading list: combat terrain dense with options, the beat of a fight, static advantages, needless precision, ambushes and map knowledge.

  • Spawn System Hoony · Challenge World · December 2000

    Seven pages on spawn luck and spawn kills, why balancing the weapons never fixed them, and a call for fair starts. His later columns on promode.org turned it into fixed spawns, and CPMA's mode built on them.
